What it is

A research engine, not a chat wrapper

Ask a legal question and a language model will answer in fluent prose, cite an article, and sound certain. What it cannot tell you is whether that article says what the answer claims, or whether it was even in force on the date in question.

Lawrider is the Swiss company behind a legal research platform that has run in production since February 2026. Retrieval runs over primary sources, and every answer passes a chain of deterministic checks before a person reads it: citation validity, quote grounding, norm attribution, precedent status. Of nine such checks, exactly one calls a language model. The rest are database and graph queries, which is why their verdicts are reproducible.

Data

Swiss litigation and legislation, measured over time

The Swiss corpus is rebuilt from its sources, entscheidsuche.ch for court decisions and Fedlex for federal law, and refreshed every night. Two things matter for research: that the decisions reach back far enough to show how a line of case law formed, and that every act can be read as it stood on a given date.

Evidence

We publish the measurements, including the unflattering ones

Trustworthiness is not a claim we make about our system. It is a quantity we compute on every request and log. Over the last thirty days the grounding score averaged 93 out of 100. When it moves, we say so.

The same discipline runs through our research. Two papers, both on the gap between a citation and a verifiable one:

Current work

Point-in-Time Swiss Law

A statutory article is not one document. Article 336 of the Code of Obligations in 2015 and in 2026 are different texts under the same identifier. Fedlex publishes every consolidated version with its in-force dates; a corpus scraped from the web keeps one snapshot and throws the interval away. A model trained on it will state repealed law with total confidence, and nothing in the output marks it as dead.

  1. Ingest. Done. Every consolidated version Fedlex publishes in machine-readable form, in German, French and Italian, with source, licence, amending act and in-force interval recorded at ingest: 37,607 versions of 17,293 acts.
  2. Diff. Done. Consecutive versions compared into a machine-readable amendment ledger, so any text can be traced to what it superseded and when: roughly 25,000 article-level changes per language so far.
  3. Measure. In progress. An open benchmark and scorer that answer one question: was the answer grounded in the version of the article valid on the date asked?

Corpus and scorer will be published openly, so the numbers can be re-run by anyone who doubts them.
